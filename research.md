# Research

Our main research goals are to understand how the diversity of life is distributed on Earth, how these patterns are affected by environmental changes both natural and caused by humans, and how these changes in biodiversity affect how ecosystems function and ultimately provide services people depend on.

In the Macroecology Lab, we research many different taxa and tend to focus on large spatial scales and long time periods. We start with fundamental ecological questions often based in biogeography and use a variety of methods spanning data science and geospatial analysis to answer them.

Here are some of our current research themes:

    <b>New applications for species distribution models</b>. 
    Species distribution models (SDMs or ENMs) estimate niche parameters and 
    make predictions of potential distribution based on species occurrence data 
    and environmental variables. These maps form the bases for many studies in 
    ecology and evolution. Kass manages the ongoing development of the R package
    ENMeval, which automates complexity tuning of SDMs. Our lab also explores 
    emerging uses of SDM predictions such as stacking them to estimate 
    biodiversity patterns, incorporating mechanistic information to improve 
    projection realism, and developing new ways to quantify and visualize 
    methodological uncertainty, among others.

    <b>Predicting ecosystem service potential with biodiversity models</b>. 
    Future climate change will alter current biodiversity patterns and affect 
    the distributions of species that provide crucial ecosystem functions and 
    services, such as pollination and food provision. We thus need accurate 
    predictions of how the diversity of these species will shift in the future, 
    including individual ranges but also estimations of biodiversity at the 
    community level. Given the connections between biodiversity and ecosystem 
    function, we can link biodiversity predictions to the potential for 
    provision of ecosystem services. Future predictions of biodiversity are 
    associated with many sources of uncertainty, and identifying which regions 
    have predictions with high confidence is crucial for environmental planning, 
    so we additionally investigate better ways to quantify this uncertainty and
    communicate it in our results.
  </div>
  
  <div>
    <b>Estimating and mapping functional diversity</b>. 
    Functional diversity is the variety of functional traits within a community. 
    These traits can include body size, body shape, food preference, life 
    history, etc., and higher trait variance means more diversity of functional 
    roles that produce ecosystem functions such as pollination, decomposition, 
    and nutrient cycling. Functional diversity is related to species richness
    but there are still differences. For example, a species-rich community 
    can have low functional diversity if all the traits are relatively similar,
    and vice versa. We use different approaches to calculate functional 
    diversity for communities based on data for measured traits, then link these 
    estimates to community composition maps predicted by species distribution 
    models. This gives us maps of functional diversity that we can then compare 
    to richness maps to uncover important geographic patterns in biodiversity.
  </div>

    <b>Calculating essential biodiversity variables for the Asia-Pacific region</b>. 
    Essential biodiversity variables (EBVs) are standardized measurements of 
    different aspects of biodiversity that can be easily compared across the 
    globe. They were envisioned by Group on Earth Observations Biodiversity 
    Observation Network (GEO BON), who oversees global efforts to monitor 
    biodiversity. As part of Dr. Kass's work with the Asia-Pacific BON, he is 
    helping to develop EBVs for this region, which is currently lacking 
    coordinated efforts to measure and monitor biodiversity due to unique
    challenges that countries in this region face. In order to get accurate
    estimates of how biodiversity is changing globally, we need to develop
    workflows to mobilize Asia-Pacific data and employ cutting-edge techniques
    to calculate variables like species distributions, diversity indices, 
    effective population sizes, and phenology.

    <b>Estimating patterns of marine biodiversity using eDNA</b>. Marine biodiversity is heavily studied but still relatively unknown. One main reason is that many organisms are very difficult to detect due to cryptic habitat preferences or behavior, occupancy of deep ocean strata, or simply because they are rare within their ranges.   <div>
    <b>Automated wildlife monitoring using cameras, sound recorders, and local 
    climate measurements</b>. Expanding networks to monitor biodiversity is 
    vital to fill sampling gaps and investigate how ecological communities shift 
    over gradients of environmental change and over time. We are constructing 
    stations in pilot areas around Japan (currently Miyagi prefecture) that 
    feature automated infrared cameras, sound recorders, and devices to measure 
    local climate conditions (i.e., below-canopy temperature). We will use these 
    data to develop models to predict species' occupancy and detection 
    probabilities over environmental gradients, as well as monitor how 
    communities change over time with disturbances. To do this, we are 
    connecting with local partners in the private sector through the COI-NEXT 
    project Nature-Positive Sustainable Development Hub, funded by 
    <a href="https://www.jst.go.jp/EN/">JST</a> and managed by Prof. Michio 
    Kondoh. We are contributors to the 
    <a href="https://www.nies.go.jp/biology/snapshot_japan/en/index.html">Snapshot Japan</a> 
    project, which aims to assess the status of wildlife in Japan on a yearly 
    basis by combining camera trap data from across the country.
